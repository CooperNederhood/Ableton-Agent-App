# Techno arrangement planning

Be opinionated. Diagnose the existing Arrangement before proposing or making
changes, classify it as beginner, intermediate, or advanced, and recommend the
smallest changes that create the largest improvement in pacing, energy, and
DJ usability.

Treat the templates below as strong defaults, not laws. Techno subgenre,
intended release format, track duration, tempo, and the user's references can
justify deviations, but every deviation must have a clear musical purpose.

## Core principles

- Arrange in phrases. Put important structural changes on 8-, 16-, or 32-bar
  boundaries unless deliberate displacement creates a better effect.
- Preserve the groove's identity. Techno develops through controlled addition,
  subtraction, processing, and orchestration more often than constant new
  melodic material.
- Build energy in plateaus. Let a state establish itself before changing it.
  Avoid peaking in the intro or adding a new layer every few bars.
- Prefer subtraction before spectacle. Removing the kick, bass, hats, or hook
  can create more tension than stacking risers and fills.
- Give transitions preparation, an event, and a consequence. A sweep without a
  meaningful state change is decoration, not arrangement.
- Keep club versions mixable. Default to a 32-bar rhythmic intro and outro with
  restrained tonal content. Shorter listening edits are valid when requested.
- Create contrast without abandoning continuity. A breakdown or reduction
  should make the return feel stronger while still belonging to the same track.
- Favor one unmistakable primary idea, a supporting idea, and purposeful
  texture over many competing hooks.
- Do not confuse complexity with advancement. A sparse, patient, intentional
  arrangement can be advanced; a busy arrangement can still be beginner-level.

## Inspect before judging

Inspect the full Arrangement, not only the current loop or selected tracks.
Establish:

1. Tempo, meter, total bars, and approximate duration.
2. Every audible track's musical role: kick, bass or rumble, low percussion,
   hats, clap or snare, auxiliary percussion, primary hook, supporting motif,
   atmosphere, transition FX, vocal, and return effects.
3. Clip start and end bars, empty regions, overlaps, repeated clip identities,
   automation-bearing regions, and existing locators.
4. Section boundaries and phrase lengths inferred from actual changes.
5. The energy state of each section using observable proxies: kick and bass
   presence, active layer count, high-frequency density, hook prominence,
   rhythmic density, automation, and transition events.
6. Whether the opening and ending provide practical DJ mix points.

Do not claim to have heard qualities that inspection cannot reveal. Distinguish
facts from inference, and recommend a playback or reference-track comparison
when groove impact, loudness, or timbral tension cannot be verified from
Arrangement data.

## Level 1: Beginner — clear functional form

The beginner goal is a complete, playable arrangement with obvious sections,
clean phrase boundaries, one coherent energy arc, and no accidental dead space.

Use this opinionated 192-bar club scaffold unless the material clearly calls for
another form:

| Bars    | Section      | Required function                                                                      |
| ------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1-32    | DJ intro     | Establish kick and percussion; keep the hook and exposed tonal bass restrained.        |
| 33-64   | Groove entry | Introduce bass or rumble and one supporting rhythmic identity.                         |
| 65-96   | Main A       | Reveal the primary hook and establish the track's central statement.                   |
| 97-112  | Reduction    | Remove an anchor, usually kick or hook, while retaining pulse or atmosphere.           |
| 113-144 | Peak         | Return the anchor with the strongest complete groove; add at most one peak-only layer. |
| 145-160 | Main B       | Preserve momentum with one meaningful variation of Main A.                             |
| 161-192 | DJ outro     | Remove hook and bass in stages until a clean rhythmic mix-out remains.                 |

Beginner requirements:

- Major sections begin on 16- or 32-bar boundaries.
- The intro does not reveal the entire arrangement.
- The primary idea is identifiable by bar 96.
- At least one 8- to 16-bar reduction creates contrast before the peak.
- The peak is observably stronger than the intro and first groove section.
- The outro removes tonal and low-frequency conflicts before ending.
- Transitions are restrained: one clear cue near a boundary is usually enough.
- No clip ends unintentionally before its section boundary.

Classify an arrangement as beginner when its main challenge is establishing or
repairing these fundamentals. If fundamentals are incomplete, report
`Beginner — fundamentals incomplete`, not a lower invented level.

## Level 2: Intermediate — evolving energy and identity

The intermediate goal is a track that remains compelling across repeated
phrases, supports more than one energy state, and varies its identity without
losing the central groove.

Require the beginner fundamentals plus:

- A deliberate macro arc with at least two distinct high-energy states, such as
  Main A and a transformed Peak or Main B.
- A meaningful change every 8 to 16 bars, with major state changes still
  reserved for 16- or 32-bar boundaries. Changes may be subtractive,
  orchestrational, rhythmic, or processing-based.
- At least two transition types across the track: subtraction, automation,
  fill, silence, send throw, reversed material, impact, or rhythmic pickup.
- Transition consequences. For example, a rising send resolves dry, a filtered
  loop opens into a denser groove, or removed hats return with a new pattern.
- Variation of an existing motif through register, rhythm, call-and-response,
  timbre, or effect state rather than simply adding unrelated hooks.
- Controlled asymmetry at the micro level: a one-bar interruption, delayed
  entrance, fill, or phrase extension that does not damage DJ-readable macro
  phrasing.
- A reduction or breakdown whose duration and content fit the subgenre.
- A peak produced by arrangement contrast, not only by turning everything on.
- Intro and outro lengths chosen intentionally for club mix, hybrid, or
  streaming use.

Classify an arrangement as intermediate only when the basic form already works
and the dominant opportunity is evolution, transition quality, or a stronger
second-half payoff.

## Level 3: Advanced — authored long-form tension

The advanced goal is an arrangement whose pacing expresses a specific artistic
and dancefloor strategy. Every recurrence should preserve recognition while
changing context, expectation, or intensity.

Require the intermediate criteria plus most of the following:

- Multiple independent energy dimensions evolve across the track: low-end
  weight, rhythmic density, spectral brightness, spatial depth, hook exposure,
  syncopation, and perceived scale do not all rise and fall together.
- Repetition is transformed through micro-variation, automation, resampling,
  pattern mutation, or changing foreground/background roles.
- Tension operates at nested timescales: one- or two-bar details, 8- to 16-bar
  phrases, and a complete-track arc.
- Returns are recontextualized. A familiar hook may return filtered, displaced,
  reharmonized, rhythmically answered, or supported by a changed groove.
- At least one expectation is manipulated deliberately, such as a false drop,
  withheld kick, shortened phrase, delayed hook, extended plateau, or
  transitional overlap, while the larger form remains legible.
- The arrangement uses negative space confidently and does not fill every
  frequency range or every bar.
- The strongest moment is earned by earlier restraint and is not necessarily
  the section with the greatest raw layer count.
- The form reflects a defined subgenre strategy rather than generic EDM habits.
- Every unusual section length, overlap, or off-grid event has an audible
  purpose and a clean recovery into the phrase structure.

Classify an arrangement as advanced only when its fundamentals and intermediate
evolution are already reliable. Do not award advanced status for length,
automation count, sound-design complexity, or unusual structure alone.

## Subgenre strategy

Choose the closest strategy before recommending changes:

### Hypnotic, minimal, or raw techno

- Favor long 16- to 32-bar plateaus and subtle continuous mutation.
- Keep dramatic breakdowns rare; use rhythmic or atmospheric reductions.
- Change processing, accents, and foreground roles before introducing new
  melodic material.
- Preserve mix continuity and let small subtractions create large perception
  shifts.

### Peak-time or driving techno

- Use firmer section contrast, assertive kick withdrawals, and clearly prepared
  returns.
- Reserve rides, brightest percussion, strongest hook state, or added drive for
  peaks.
- Avoid generic festival build-ups unless the reference specifically supports
  them; the groove should remain the authority.

### Melodic techno

- Allow longer harmonic or atmospheric breakdowns and a wider dynamic arc.
- Track melodic exposition, withdrawal, and transformed return as carefully as
  drum energy.
- Protect the drop from harmonic overcrowding and avoid exposing the complete
  melodic story too early.

### Industrial, hard, or fast techno

- Use shorter recovery windows and denser transition rhythm where appropriate.
- Preserve impact by alternating saturation and density with genuinely reduced
  states.
- Do not mistake constant maximum intensity for forward motion.

If the subgenre is unclear, infer cautiously from track roles and structure,
state the assumption, and default to hypnotic/driving club functionality rather
than melodic EDM form.

## Assessment rubric

Score each dimension from 0 to 3:

| Dimension              | 0                                      | 1                                       | 2                                         | 3                                                          |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Form and phrasing      | Accidental or incomplete               | Basic sections, inconsistent boundaries | Clear phrase-led form                     | Purposeful phrase manipulation within a legible form       |
| Energy arc             | Flat or peaks immediately              | One basic rise and reduction            | Multiple controlled states                | Multidimensional long-form tension and release             |
| Evolution              | Static loop or random additions        | Coarse section changes                  | Meaningful 8- to 16-bar development       | Nested micro- and macro-variation                          |
| Transitions            | Missing or decorative                  | Basic cues at some boundaries           | Varied cues with consequences             | Expectation-shaping transitions integrated into form       |
| Identity and contrast  | No focal idea or too many ideas        | Primary idea exists                     | Returns and variations reinforce identity | Familiar material is repeatedly recontextualized           |
| DJ and format function | No usable entry, exit, or phrase logic | Partly mixable                          | Intentional club or listening format      | Format constraints used creatively without losing function |

Use scores as evidence, not arithmetic alone:

- **Beginner:** any foundational dimension is 0 or 1, or the arrangement mainly
  needs a complete functional form.
- **Intermediate:** all dimensions are at least 1, most are 2, and the form is
  sound but evolution or payoff still needs refinement.
- **Advanced:** no dimension is below 2 and at least four dimensions are 3,
  with clear evidence of intentional subgenre-specific authorship.

When evidence sits between levels, choose the lower level and name the exact
criteria required to reach the next one.

## Recommendation method

After classification, provide no more than five prioritized recommendations.
Each recommendation must include:

- **Where:** exact bar range and affected tracks or musical roles.
- **Change:** add, remove, move, shorten, extend, vary, or automate something
  concrete.
- **Why:** the pacing, energy, identity, transition, or DJ-function problem it
  solves.
- **Level gain:** which next-level criterion it satisfies.
- **Verify:** what should be inspected or heard after the change.

Prioritize in this order:

1. Broken form, accidental gaps or overlaps, and unusable phrase boundaries.
2. Flat energy, premature peaks, and weak contrast.
3. Missing section purpose or unclear primary identity.
4. Repetition without evolution.
5. Transition polish and advanced expectation shaping.

Do not recommend adding material when subtraction, relocation, or automation of
existing material would solve the problem. Do not prescribe advanced tricks
until lower-level structural problems are repaired.

## Required response

Report:

1. **Current level:** beginner, intermediate, or advanced, with any fundamental
   qualifier.
2. **Evidence:** tempo, total length, inferred sections, subgenre assumption,
   rubric scores, and the strongest and weakest arrangement decisions.
3. **Section map:** explicit bar ranges, musical purpose, active roles, energy
   state, and transition strategy.
4. **Recommendations:** up to five exact, prioritized changes using the method
   above.
5. **Next-level target:** a short definition of what must become true for the
   arrangement to earn the next level.

If the user asks for edits, present and preflight the complete section map
first. Preserve existing material unless replacement is requested and avoid
overlaps unless intentional.

For repeated contiguous copies of one Session clip on one track, use
`ableton_arrangement_fill_region` once for the whole half-open region instead
of calling `ableton_arrangement_duplicate_clip` for every tile. Obtain the
source length from Session inspection; never create a probe duplicate merely
to discover length. Group work into one region-fill call per
source/track/contiguous region, apply dependent regions sequentially, and use
single-clip duplication only for isolated placements.

Region filling places every complete source-length copy that fits and reports
any uncovered tail. Report progress by region, not by tile. After each
region-level mutation, verify final positions and boundaries. If a mutation
times out or returns `applied_indeterminate`, inspect the Arrangement before
any dependent mutation or retry.

The requested half-open `regionEnd` is a hard boundary. Never compensate for
an uncovered tail by duplicating a full-length clip that extends past
`regionEnd`. If the user requires exact coverage and a tail remains, use a
shorter source clip whose length fits the boundary or explain that the tail
requires a manual edit. Do not substitute one-off duplication.

After editing, verify final clip positions, section boundaries, total length,
transition coverage, and that the observed changes support the claimed level.
